\section{Executive Summary}

This report provides a preliminary review of the Weather Enhancement Technology Array (WETA)
installed by Rain Enhancement Technologies (RET) on the Flat Iron Mesa, outside Moab, Utah.
WETA is a self-sufficient and ``off grid'' solution installed to provide enhancement of precipitation,
including snowfall during the cold season and rainfall during the warm season. The present report
covers October 2026, the first month of the new water year and the first month after the
April--September warm season.

\subsection{Operational Summary}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item This report covers 31 days from 10/01/2026 to 10/31/2026.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
    \item WETA operated on fifteen days in October 2026.
    \item Precipitation was observed on eighteen days in October at one or more La Sal SNOTEL sites.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item October 2026 was the wettest October in the comparison record (2013--2025; 2021--2025
  at Gold Basin) at all three La Sal sites. The Abajo controls were wetter still, so all three
  target-minus-control differences were negative. The month was exceptionally wet across the
  region, and a single month does not support definitive attribution.
  \item Ridge, BSTS, and analog-year estimates for October precipitation and snow water
  equivalent (Section~\ref{sec:estimates}) do not indicate enhancement at La Sal Upper or
  La Sal Lower.
  \item The first snow of the season was recorded in mid-October. Snow cover was shallow and
  short-lived at La Sal Upper and La Sal Lower and was still present at Gold Basin at the end
  of the month. Because the snowpack was only beginning to form, this report continues to
  focus on accumulated precipitation.
  \item The longest continuous operating period was 7 days, from 10/07/2026 to 10/13/2026.
  That period and the next one (10/16/2026 to 10/20/2026) both ended in shutdowns for flood
  risk (0908 on 10/13 and 1300 on 10/20).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Actions and Improvements}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item This report evaluates October precipitation using the same methods applied during the
  warm season, including comparisons between observations in the La Sal Mountains
  and selected reference stations from the NRCS SNOTEL network. Candidate stations were
  assessed based on historical correlation with the La Sal target sites, geographic relationship
  to the treatment area, elevation and climatic setting, and consistency of the available
  precipitation records. Primary controls are Abajo Mountains SNOTEL pairs
  (Buckboard Flat and Camp Jackson).
  \item Installation of additional instrumentation in the La Sal Mountains is planned for this summer,
  pending the necessary USDA Forest Service permitting and approvals.
  \item Methods of evaluation for warm-season precipitation are being tested presently, including
  incorporation of data from the Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor (MRMS) dataset and secondary
  consistency checks against NWS Cooperative Observer Program (COOP) stations.
  \item WETA operations will continue to target predicted precipitation events that are not considered
  adverse weather conditions.
  \item An automatic shut-off capability for lightning events occurring within a 6-mile radius of WETA
  is currently under development as a protection system for the WETA.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data and Analysis}

\subsection{Summary of Precipitation Events}

Figure~\ref{fig:precip} presents the daily time-series of precipitation measured at SNOTEL sites in
the region. October precipitation was heavy and fell mainly in two multi-day storms, on
10--14 October and 21--22 October, with smaller events on 24--25 and 30--31 October. The
10--14 October storm produced about half of the month's precipitation at each La Sal site,
including the largest daily totals (about 1.7~in at La Sal Upper and Gold Basin on

morning of 13 October, and was off during the 21--22 October storm following the

\subsection{Evaluation of Precipitation Enhancement}
\label{sec:evaluation}
 
Enhancement is evaluated by comparing accumulated precipitation at target sites in the La Sal
Mountains against control sites outside the treatment area, using records from the SNOTEL
locations that have supported this program since its inception. SNOTEL precipitation is measured
with an all-season storage gauge (Fleming et al., 2023), which reports year-round and is therefore
suitable for warm-season (April--September; hereafter ``warm season'') rainfall analysis. Candidate
control sites were drawn from two observing networks (NRCS SNOTEL and NWS COOP) and assessed on
three joint criteria: correlation of April--September monthly precipitation totals with the target,
consistency of instrumentation with the target gauges, and geographic position relative to the
treatment area.

The Abajo Mountains provide the primary controls. Buckboard Flat and Camp Jackson
are among the most strongly correlated candidates for every La Sal target over the
warm season, employ the same all-season storage-gauge instrumentation as the target sites,
and lie south-southwest of the target---upwind or crosswind of the prevailing southwesterly
to westerly flow, and sufficiently removed from the intended treatment area to minimize the
likelihood of treatment influence. On this basis the analysis adopts three pairs: La Sal Upper
with Buckboard Flat, La Sal Lower with Camp Jackson (assigned on elevation match and to avoid
dependence of all pairs on a single control gauge), and Gold Basin with Buckboard Flat. These
are the pairs presented in Figures~\ref{fig:precip_month_clim} and~\ref{fig:precip_season}.

Two further groups of stations were examined and assigned supporting rather than primary roles.
Three SNOTEL sites in adjacent ranges---Columbine Pass, Lone Cone and East Willow
Creek---exhibit comparable correlations with the targets but lie farther away across separate
mountain ranges. At those separations, month-to-month agreement with the target is unlikely to
reflect the same individual storms, particularly for convective precipitation; these sites are
therefore reported as a regional reference on whether a given month was broadly wet or dry
across southeastern Utah and southwestern Colorado, not as controls. Separately, NWS
Cooperative Observer Program (COOP) stations (Arches NP HQS and Blanding) provide a
low-elevation perspective on the same events. COOP sites correlate only moderately with La Sal
Upper and La Sal Lower and are used as a secondary consistency check for those two targets
only; they lie well below the targets in semi-arid valley and desert-floor settings, employ
manually read daily gauges whose observation-time convention does not align with the automated
SNOTEL record, and are subject to observer-dependent gaps.

The same pairs and method are applied to October, the first month after the warm season.
Turning to the reporting period, the following observations are made.
 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item October 2026 was far above climatology at La Sal Upper, La Sal Lower, and Gold Basin.
        Each site recorded its wettest October of the comparison record.
  \item Precipitation at the La Sal SNOTEL sites fell mainly in two multi-day storms. WETA was
        operating during the first storm until the flood-risk shutdown and was off during the
        second.
  \item The Abajo controls were wetter still. Both exceeded their previous October maxima by
        a wide margin, so the target-minus-control differences for October 2026 were negative
        at all three pairs.
  \item Those differences sit below the climatological range at the La Sal Upper and Gold Basin
        pairs and near the bottom of the range at the La Sal Lower pair. The lowest October
        differences previously on record at all three pairs occurred in October 2025, before
        WETA operations began, which was also a wet October at the Abajo controls. No claim of
        enhancement is made for October in isolation.
  \item The regional reference gauges show the same picture. Columbine Pass, Lone Cone, and
        East Willow Creek each recorded their wettest October of the comparison record, so the
        month was exceptionally wet across the surrounding ranges, with the heaviest totals at
        the Abajo controls. The negative target-minus-control differences therefore sit
        against an exceptionally wet regional background.
  \item The low-elevation COOP check is consistent with that picture at Arches NP HQS, which was
        far wetter than its usual October. Blanding did not report during October 2026 and is
        omitted from this month's check.
  \item The Gold Basin comparison is shown for completeness, but its distribution should be
        interpreted with caution: the site entered service in October 2021, so only a few paired
        October observations are available and its climatological range is correspondingly
        poorly constrained.
  \item Viewed across April--October (Figure~\ref{fig:precip_season}), October is by far the
        wettest 2026 month at the targets and at the controls. The September and October
        differences are the most negative of the period at all three pairs, and both occurred
        in months when the Abajo controls were unusually wet.
  \item Ridge, BSTS, and analog-year estimates for October are given in
        Section~\ref{sec:estimates} and Table~\ref{tab:enhancement}. None indicates
        enhancement.
\end{enumerate}
 
Three factors currently limit the sensitivity of this evaluation. First, the sample is short:
the longest paired record for a primary pair covers a little more than a decade,
and Gold Basin has only a few overlapping years. Second, warm-season precipitation in this region is dominated by convective events
whose spatial scale is smaller than the target--control separation, so a single control gauge
samples the storm field only imperfectly---as illustrated by the month-to-month reversal of
sign at individual pairs from month to month. The October storms were multi-day events recorded
at every gauge examined, yet totals still differed widely between neighboring ranges, so the
same caution applies. Third, month-to-month storm placement can
reverse the sign of a single-pair difference even when regional totals are elevated. To address
these, subsequent reports will (i) aggregate differences across full seasons
rather than relying on isolated months, (ii) incorporate gridded Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor (MRMS)
quantitative precipitation estimates to characterize precipitation over the target area as a
whole rather than at points, and (iii) extend the gauge network through the additional
instrumentation planned for installation in the La Sal Mountains. It is further noted that
SNOTEL is not a homogenized climate-reference network (Fleming et al., 2023); paired differencing
is used throughout to mitigate any instrument-era discontinuities common to both sites of a
pair.

\subsection{Ridge, BSTS, and Analog-Year Estimates}
\label{sec:estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:enhancement} gives three estimates of the October 2026 departure from expected
conditions at La Sal Upper and La Sal Lower, for accumulated precipitation and for snow water
equivalent (SWE). Each estimate is the observed value minus the value expected from the paired
Abajo control, so a positive number means more precipitation or SWE at the target than
expected.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Analog years.} The expected target-minus-control difference is the mean of
        that difference in the five driest Octobers since 2013 at the La Sal sites (2014,
        2016, 2017, 2019, and 2023). The estimate is the October 2026 difference minus that
        mean. SWE is taken on 31 October.
  \item \textbf{Ridge.} A ridge regression trained on daily October data from 2013--2025
        predicts daily target precipitation, and the daily target-minus-control SWE
        difference, from control precipitation and temperature, including recent and
        accumulated control precipitation.
  \item \textbf{BSTS.} A Bayesian structural time-series model trained on the same years
        predicts the target October precipitation total from the control total, and daily
        target SWE from daily control SWE.
\end{itemize}

For Ridge and BSTS, SWE is the October mean of daily values, and the 95\% interval is the
estimate $\pm 1.96$ times the standard deviation of the leave-one-year-out errors for the
thirteen Octobers of 2013--2025. October 2026 is not used to fit either model. Ridge and BSTS
precipitation follows the daily differencing convention of the model scripts, which covers
1--30 October and leaves out precipitation that fell on 31 October; the analog-year method
uses the full October total.

None of the three methods indicates enhancement in October. For precipitation, the
analog-year estimate is negative at both sites. The Ridge estimate is positive at both sites
and the BSTS estimate is negative at both. All four of those intervals span zero, although
the BSTS interval at La Sal Upper lies almost entirely below it. For SWE, every
estimate is a few tenths of an inch or less and every interval spans zero; October snow cover
at these two sites was shallow and short-lived.

These estimates should be read with caution, for three reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The Abajo controls were far outside the range on which Ridge and BSTS were trained:
        the October 2026 total was about twice the previous October maximum at Buckboard Flat
        and about one and a half times at Camp Jackson. Both models are therefore
        extrapolating, and they disagree most at La Sal Upper, where the control was furthest
        out of range. A BSTS error larger than the La Sal Upper estimate occurred in
        October 2025, before WETA operations began.
  \item The analog years are dry Octobers, whereas October 2026 was the wettest of the
        comparison record at the La Sal sites.
        Selecting the five wettest Octobers instead leaves both precipitation estimates
        negative.
  \item The Ridge and BSTS models were developed for December--February SWE and
        April--August precipitation. They are applied to October here without separate
        validation for that month.
\end{enumerate}
